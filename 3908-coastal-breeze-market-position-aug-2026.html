Where we are

Twenty-seven days on the market. One showing. That prospect liked the property and decided the commute would not work for them. No applications.

The property is show-ready and you followed every recommendation we made. It is professionally photographed and marketed with a virtual tour, an interactive floor plan, and Realtor co-broke, which most management companies do not offer. Zillow's own data shows single-family rentals with a 3D tour receive roughly 44% more page views and 65% more saves than comparable listings without one, and renters can filter their search to show only listings that have one.

That matters here for one reason: it removes exposure as an explanation. Three things rent a property — location, condition, and price. Location cannot be changed. Condition is done. Price is the only lever left.

A note on how this set was pulled

This is a wider search than the June analysis. I extended the geographic radius and deliberately included properties that are not as well equipped as yours — homes with two or two and a half bathrooms instead of three, and two-car garages instead of three.

That was intentional, and it matters for how you read the table.

A tenant with a budget is not shopping by bathroom count. They open a search, set a maximum monthly figure, and compare everything that appears. A four-bedroom at $2,800 with two baths and a two-car garage is competing directly with your property whether or not it matches on paper. If it is in their price range and yours is not, they never see yours at all.

Read the table with that in mind. Your property has more bathrooms, more garage space, more square footage, a water view, and gated access than most of what is listed above — and it is still priced above almost all of it. The extra features are real, and they are worth something. They are not worth enough to overcome being outside a tenant's search range, because a tenant who filters at $2,800 never reaches the page where those features are listed.

Put differently: the wider net makes this analysis harder on your property, not easier. Even measured against homes with fewer baths and smaller garages, the price is not where the activity is.

Three findings

One — there is a ceiling at $3,000

Only one property in this corridor leased above $3,000, and it was a five-bedroom that leased back in April. Since then, nothing has cleared $3,000 except a single property that reached exactly $3,000 — and it took 108 days and a price reduction to get there.

Your property is the largest in the comparison set. That would normally be an advantage. In this market right now it is not translating into rent.

Three — everything priced at $2,800 and above is sitting

The two properties still unleased are at $2,800 and $2,600. One has been on the market 48 days with no offers; the other has been on 148 days and has already taken two reductions. The property that went under contract fastest in this set — 20 days — is priced at $2,500.

The homes that leased quickly were priced where tenants were already looking. The homes that are sitting were priced where owners hoped they would look.

Why the June recommendation was right, and is not right now

The June 10 analysis recommended $3,195. At that point, comparable properties in this corridor were leasing at $3,300 in 13 days and $3,000 in 36 days. That number reflected the market as it actually was.

The property entered the market on July 23 — six weeks later. The market moved in that window.

This is the nature of rental data. It can only be read in arrears. A shift becomes visible in the numbers after it has already happened, not before. That is not a shortcoming of the analysis; it is how the measurement works, and it is why pricing has to be revisited against current conditions rather than the conditions that existed when the recommendation was made.

One cause is measurable right here. Three of the nine properties in this comparison set were listed for sale, failed to sell, and were converted to rentals — including two whose sale listings were cancelled within the last three weeks. Owners in this corridor are pulling homes off the sales market and renting them instead. Every one of those becomes a direct competitor for the same tenant, and none of them appeared in a June comp set because they were not rentals yet.

Two things about that are worth stating plainly.

  • It is not your property. The decline runs across the entire portfolio, across price points and locations. A problem with one listing's photographs or marketing does not produce a portfolio-wide drop.
  • It is not the season. Our records from last year show August was busier than July, not slower. Whatever is happening now is not the usual late-summer pattern.
Why this matters more than the comp table. Leased comparables tell you what the market did weeks or months ago, because a property only appears as leased after the fact. Application volume tells you what tenants are doing this week. It moves first, and it is currently moving down sharply.

A full year of the reduction costs less than one more month of vacancy. And unlike rent that is never collected, the reduction buys something: a property that is occupied, maintained, and generating income.

The cost of waiting compounds. Every week at a price the market has already declined adds days on market, and days on market is itself a signal that prospective tenants read. A listing that has been available for two months invites lower expectations than one that is fresh.

What the reduction is meant to do

This is not simply about leasing faster. It is about creating enough interest that we have a choice.

One showing gives us no selection at all. A pool of interested applicants lets us screen properly and place the strongest qualified resident — the one with the credit, the income stability, and the rental history that protects this property over the next several years. A well-qualified resident who stays is worth far more than a marginal one who pays slightly more rent and leaves in twelve months.

Pricing to generate volume is how we earn the right to be selective.
